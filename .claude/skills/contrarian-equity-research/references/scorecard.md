# Optional Evidence Scorecard

Use this rubric only to make reasoning consistent and auditable. It is not a validated forecasting model and is not part of any publicly documented official Geert Schaaij/Beursgenoten system. Do not treat totals as probabilities, expected returns, or investment instructions. A high quality score cannot compensate automatically for an excessive price; a low score is not a short recommendation.

## Scoring anchors

Score each applicable dimension from 1 to 5:

- **1 — Materially weak:** evidence points to persistent deterioration, fragile finances, poor economics, or serious unresolved risks.
- **2 — Below average / concern:** meaningful shortcomings outweigh strengths; evidence is incomplete or trends are negative.
- **3 — Mixed / adequate:** neither clearly attractive nor clearly weak; important counterevidence exists.
- **4 — Strong:** positive multi-period evidence, with manageable risks and credible supporting disclosures.
- **5 — Exceptional:** unusually strong and durable evidence across cycles or relevant peers; not merely one favorable quarter.

Use **NA** if there is insufficient reliable evidence or the criterion does not fit the business model. Explain why. Do not impute NA as 3 and do not include NA in averages.

## Dimensions and what evidence qualifies

1. **Growth quality:** sustained organic growth, repeatability, healthy mix, per-share growth, and plausible demand drivers. Penalize acquisition-only growth or growth purchased with uneconomic spending.
2. **Profitability and returns:** margin level/trend, ROIC/ROE appropriate to the sector, and evidence of durable economics. Evaluate multi-year trends and explain unusual volatility.
3. **Cash conversion:** free-cash-flow generation and conversion of earnings to cash over time. Consider working capital, capital expenditure, and dilution. Do not penalize necessary investment without examining returns.
4. **Balance-sheet resilience:** net debt, maturity schedule, liquidity, interest coverage, leases and contingent obligations relative to the business's cash-flow stability. Use sector context.
5. **Competitive durability:** evidence that the company can defend pricing, customers, cost position, licenses, network, brand, or other advantage. Score the trend as well as the present state.
6. **Management and capital allocation:** credibility against past guidance, governance, dilution, acquisitions, buybacks/dividends, reinvestment discipline, and transparency.
7. **Valuation attractiveness:** current price relative to a defensible value range, suitable peers, and the company's own history. Account for growth, cyclicality, leverage, and risks. This is a valuation score, not a quality score.
8. **Catalyst evidence:** specificity, verifiability, timing, and causal link to changed cash flow, risk, or market expectations. A vague hope or macro forecast does not score highly.
9. **Downside / thesis resilience:** score higher when permanent-loss pathways are limited and controllable, lower when downside is severe, asymmetric, or inadequately understood. Explain the direction explicitly, because this dimension is reverse-coded versus “risk severity.”
10. **Evidence quality:** primary-source coverage, data recency, consistency, and ability to reconcile key facts. Score low when the investment case depends on promotional sources, unverified estimates, stale data, or unverifiable claims.

## How to summarize without false precision

Prefer a dimension-by-dimension table in the report showing score, evidence, confidence, and key unknown. If an aggregate is useful, report **fundamentals quality** (dimensions 1–6) separately from **valuation/opportunity** (7–9) and **evidence quality** (10). Show simple arithmetic only if no dimension is NA, disclose that weights are equal, and state that the summary is descriptive, not predictive. Do not create a combined recommendation threshold such as “over 4 means buy.”

An alternative is a qualitative status per dimension: positive, mixed, concern, or not assessed. This is often better for companies with heterogeneous segments or unstable data. In all cases explain at least one material counterargument to the thesis.

## Confidence labels

- **High:** key facts confirmed with recent primary sources, consistent data definitions, and adequate history.
- **Medium:** most important facts verified, but some material estimates, limited history, or mixed evidence remain.
- **Low:** major source gaps, stale or conflicting data, poor comparability, high uncertainty, or reliance on estimates.

Confidence measures confidence in the analysis inputs and interpretation, not the likelihood of a positive return.

## Company-specific gates

Regardless of score, do not characterize a security as an attractive opportunity without resolving material red flags such as going-concern uncertainty, likely covenant breach, near-term refinancing risk, credible fraud allegations, major accounting restatement, severe dilution, inability to verify ownership/share count, or a potentially terminal regulatory event. If unresolved, state “watch / insufficient evidence” and describe the evidence needed.

For cyclicals, use normalized mid-cycle earnings and avoid scoring peak margins as durable. For banks and insurers, adapt cash-flow and leverage metrics; regulatory capital, asset quality, underwriting, reserves, and book value may matter more than generic industrial-company ratios. For REITs, evaluate FFO/AFFO, debt, lease terms, occupancy, and NAV. For early-stage or loss-making firms, do not apply standard P/E; focus on unit economics, funding runway, dilution, and milestones, and mark valuation confidence low if assumptions dominate.
